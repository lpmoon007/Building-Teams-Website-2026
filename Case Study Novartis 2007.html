The ‘failure’ that became our playbook.

Updated August 2026

In 2007 we ran a bike build for 1,000 Novartis people in an unfinished Dallas basement, sure the sound system would fail. It did — and the two changes we made to survive it reshaped every large event we’ve run since.

In short

At a 2007 Novartis national meeting for 1,000 people in an unfinished, open concrete basement in Dallas, we suspected the room’s size and acoustics would kill the sound system. In a build that big, a room that can’t hear the facilitators turns into chaos. So we made two changes — complete written instructions at every table, and volunteers quietly pre-paired with the children before the reveal. The sound did fail. It barely mattered. Those two fixes became the operating playbook for every large-scale event we’ve run since.

The challenge

Novartis brought 1,000 people together for a national meeting in Dallas. The build space was an unfinished, open concrete basement — cavernous, hard-surfaced, and acoustically brutal. We looked at the room and suspected the truth: the sound system probably wouldn’t carry.

That is a serious problem at scale. A give-back build for a thousand people depends on everyone hearing the same instruction at the same moment. Lose the sound, and you lose the room — a thousand people improvising at once is how a meaningful event turns into a scramble.

What we did · the main change

We rebuilt the event so it didn’t depend on the stage at all. Every table received complete written instructions: exactly who does what, the timing of each step, where to go for help, and even where the trash goes — with our staff in bright orange shirts as the obvious people to flag down. If the microphone died, each table could still run its own build, start to finish, without missing a beat.

What we did · the second change

The first change unlocked the second. Instead of pairing children to teams from the stage after the build — the classic large-group chaos of “okay, which team doesn’t have a special guest?” — we quietly pulled one volunteer from each team, without telling them why.

Those volunteers went to meet the children first, help them feel comfortable, and then walk them straight to their own team — no announcement, no scramble, no 250 kids searching a room of a thousand adults. The reveal simply arrived, table by table, exactly where it belonged.

The result

The sound system failed, right on cue — and it barely registered. The room ran itself. The amount of chaos in the event dropped massively, and 250 children each met the team that built their bike, calmly and personally.

We walked out having learned the single most valuable operational lesson in our history: design every large build to run without the stage. We’ve carried both changes into every event since — including a Guinness World Record build for 5,000 people.

“The sound system failed — and it became the most valuable thing we ever learned about running big events. This ‘failure’ may be the biggest success we’ve ever had.”
James Carter · Founder & lead facilitator, Building Teams
The playbook it created

How we keep 1,000+ people from feeling chaotic.

Novartis 2007 is why our large events don’t rely on a working microphone or a flawless PA. Two principles, still standard on every big build:

  • ✓The table can run itself — complete written instructions, timing, help points and orange-shirted staff at every table.
  • ✓The reveal comes to the team — children are pre-paired and walked to their own table, never announced and scrambled for.
  • ✓Redundancy by design — the event survives a failed sound system, a delayed start, or a room that fights you.

It’s the discipline behind our biggest builds — see DaVita’s four-room reveal for 2,200 and our conference team building.
